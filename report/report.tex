\begin{abstract}
Integration-by-parts reduction expresses Feynman integrals in a master basis. We train models to choose seeds and equations before Kira performs exact algebra, and compare complete reduction time with matched controls. Earlier equation-filter tests give 4.8\% less time on 68 matched batches at one through four loops; a retained sparse-seed workflow gives 18.0\% less time on 24 reserved two-loop requests. Learned row selection remains omitted because its tests show no reliable gain. We extend seed-cost training to vacuum, two-point, and scattering families. On 41 new matched batches with validation-selected exact routes, the combined-to-previous application ratio is 0.961, with repeat interval [0.934, 1.021]. The new target-dependent model deploys on 1 matched batch, with learned-to-fixed time ratio 0.839 under the same exact implementation; the learned-to-native ratio for this batch is 1.006. Inactive domains contribute timing variation to the all-batch learned comparison. We report each family separately and retain failures. The calculations keep the dimension symbolic and use rational kinematics and supplied master bases; timings include prediction, equation generation, algebra, exact checks, and retries. Separate exact changes reduce sector-storage requirements and admit full-coordinate scalar massless two-point inputs through twelve tested loops. These scalar checks do not use ML. The evidence establishes gains within stated domains and does not establish a learned speedup for every integral type or loop order.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Higher-order perturbative corrections to scattering amplitudes and particle decay amplitudes require integrals over loop momenta. As the loop order increases, a calculation produces many integrals with related numerator and denominator structures, and integration-by-parts (IBP) identities allow them to be expressed in terms of a smaller set of master integrals. This reduction is an essential step in many multiloop calculations in quantum field theory~\cite{Weinzierl2022,ChetyrkinTkachov}.

Kira automates the reduction by generating algebraic identities and solving the resulting linear system~\cite{Laporta,KiraOriginal}. Although the identities are exact, the amount of work depends on which equations are generated and how the system is solved. Modern Kira versions already use improved seed and equation selection to reduce unnecessary work~\cite{Kira3}. We call the requested integrals the targets and ask whether models trained on earlier reductions can reduce the time needed to express them in a master basis.

We investigate three places where a learned choice can affect the calculation: selecting the seeds from which IBP identities are generated, selecting the row used in an elimination step, and selecting individual equations before their symbolic coefficients are constructed. Earlier work has used machine-assisted search to discover seed-selection rules~\cite{Song2025}. Here we assess each model through the time required to complete the reduction, including prediction, exact checks, and any repeated calculation after an unsuccessful choice. This criterion matters because an accurate prediction or a smaller equation set can still lead to a slower calculation.

The main comparisons separate two levels of decision. We first train models to choose the bounds of the seed region, using measured complete reduction times as labels. Tuned fixed bounds explain nearly all of that saving. We therefore hold those bounds fixed and train shared and graph-specific classification trees to select individual equations or whole seeds inside the region. This second comparison measures the additional effect of learned filtering. Earlier tests of row selection, exact preprocessing, and restricted high-loop recurrences provide the separate evidence needed to identify where each saving comes from.

The next comparison trains a model to choose which targets Kira reduces together. This choice changes the seed limits of each group and can trade repeated preparation for a smaller equation system. We compare its complete time with the preceding undivided workflow and with separately tuned fixed partitions.

We then train a seed boundary directly from complete Kira times. This model changes which seeds generate identities, and a second model predicts which fitted boundary to use for a target. The comparison includes tuned native Kira, a fixed sparse boundary, and the preceding application. It therefore tests both the benefit of a learned region and the additional value of selecting a region for each request.

\section{Feynman integrals and their reduction with Kira}
\label{sec:reduction}
\subsection{Families, indices, and sectors}
An integral family specifies a set of propagators and the external scalar products. Integrals in the same family differ in the integer powers assigned to those propagators~\cite{Weinzierl2022,KiraOriginal}:
\begin{equation}
 I_{\boldsymbol a}(\boldsymbol x,d)
 =\int\prod_{h=1}^{L}\frac{d^d\ell_h}{i\pi^{d/2}}
       \prod_{i=1}^{N}D_i^{-a_i},
 \qquad D_i=q_i^2-m_i^2+i0.
 \label{eq:family}
\end{equation}
Here $L$ is the number of loops, $\ell_h$ is the momentum of loop $h$, and $N$ is the number of inverse propagators, including any auxiliary entries. The momentum $q_i$ is a linear combination of loop and external momenta, and $m_i$ is the corresponding mass. The term $i0$ specifies the Feynman prescription. We use dimensional regularization with $d=4-2\epsilon$, where $\epsilon$ is the regulator, and collect the independent momentum invariants and squared masses in $\boldsymbol x$.

The index $a_i$ determines how $D_i$ enters the integrand. A positive index places it in the denominator, a zero index removes it, and a negative index places a power of it in the numerator. Auxiliary inverse propagators represent scalar products that cannot be expressed using the physical propagators alone. Integrals with the same set of positive indices belong to the same \emph{sector}~\cite{KiraOriginal}. A top sector specifies a maximal declared set of positive propagator indices, and its subsectors follow by removing propagators~\cite{KiraOriginal}. These definitions give Kira a common way to label the integrals and organize the equations between them.

\subsection{From an IBP identity to a master-integral reduction}
An IBP identity starts from an index vector $\boldsymbol\sigma$, called a \emph{seed}. We multiply its integrand by a loop or external momentum $v$, differentiate with respect to a loop momentum $\ell_k$, and integrate the total derivative. Dimensional regularization gives~\cite{ChetyrkinTkachov}
\begin{equation}
 0=\int\prod_h\frac{d^d\ell_h}{i\pi^{d/2}}
 \frac{\partial}{\partial\ell_k^\mu}
 \left(v^\mu\prod_iD_i^{-\sigma_i}\right)
 =\sum_j A_{\boldsymbol\sigma,o;j}(\boldsymbol x,d)
     I_{\boldsymbol a^{(j)}}.
 \label{eq:ibp}
\end{equation}
The label $o$ specifies the derivative through the choices of $k$ and $v$, while $j$ labels the integrals produced when the derivative is expanded. The coefficient $A_{\boldsymbol\sigma,o;j}$ multiplies integral $I_{\boldsymbol a^{(j)}}$ in that identity. Since several operators can act on the same seed, one seed generally generates several equations. Appendix~\ref{app:ibp} derives the shifts in the integral indices and the row operation used to eliminate an integral.

Kira generates these equations within prescribed index limits, applies symmetry relations, removes redundant equations, and solves the remaining system~\cite{KiraOriginal,Kira3}. Each equation forms a row of the coefficient matrix, and each integral corresponds to a column. Elimination combines rows to remove integrals in the chosen order; back-substitution then expresses the requested targets in terms of the remaining masters. Kira can manipulate symbolic rational functions directly or use exact arithmetic modulo a prime, with reconstruction when a symbolic result is required~\cite{KiraOriginal,Kira2}. Arithmetic modulo a prime takes place in a finite field, so its numerical appearance does not introduce floating-point error.

A requested target may be a single indexed integral or a linear combination $T=\sum_u c_u I_{\boldsymbol a^{(u)}}$. The desired result is
\begin{equation}
 T=\sum_{m=1}^{n_M}r_{Tm}(\boldsymbol x,d)M_m,
 \qquad r_{Tm}\in\Q(\boldsymbol x,d).
 \label{eq:master}
\end{equation}
Here $c_u$ is the coefficient of a target term, $M_m$ is master integral $m$, and $n_M$ is the number of masters. The field $\Q(\boldsymbol x,d)$ consists of rational functions of the invariants, squared masses, and dimension with rational-number coefficients. Reduction determines the coefficients $r_{Tm}$; evaluating the master integrals is a separate part of the amplitude calculation.

\subsection{Where learned choices can reduce the work}
Table~\ref{tab:decisions} identifies the three decisions studied here. Seed selection changes which groups of identities are available, row selection changes the elimination steps, and equation selection can suppress an individual seed--operator pair before Kira constructs its coefficients. We use \emph{native choices} to mean Kira's own decision rules. A learned model changes a decision, while Kira continues to carry out the exact algebra.

Every completed target must reach the prescribed master basis. In the comparisons, we also check its coefficients against an independently obtained exact reduction. If a chosen seed set or equation set is insufficient, the calculation repeats with native choices or a larger seed region. We call this repeated attempt a \emph{fallback} and include its cost in the reported time. Thus the relevant question for each model is whether the work it avoids exceeds the cost of prediction and any fallback.

\subsection{Exact reuse in the restricted vacuum family}
The high-loop vacuum study explores a different source of acceleration. For the family in Appendix~\ref{app:vacuum}, exact recurrences express the reduction coefficients through $z=L(d-2)$. A coefficient calculation can then serve several loop orders and several targets. The method reuses intermediate coefficients and checks each result with an independent Taylor expansion; Kira remains available if this direct check fails. The deployed recurrence uses a fixed rule. Its large saving comes from exact mathematical reuse and implementation changes. A separately trained selector for recurrence strategies takes 1.7\% more time than the fixed rule and supplies no established additional saving.

The comparison uses eight fresh groups, each containing three mass profiles with two, three, or four massive denominators, and requests loop orders one through twelve. Five repetitions compare the saved original Kira 3.1 executable, a later full-pool control, and the recurrence method on identical requests. All 120 calculations pass exact checks. The final-to-original time ratio is $0.001839$, corresponding to speedup $543.815$ with interval $[488.134,605.064]$. A separate check verifies 27,960 nonzero target--master coefficient polynomials. The successful recurrence calculations need no Kira calls, whereas each baseline makes 1,440 calls across its trials.

\section{Training data and experimental design}
\label{sec:data}
\subsection{Constructing targets with known reductions}
To train the original models, we need targets together with master coefficients and a recorded sequence of valid reduction steps. We obtain these data by working backwards from checked master relations. If $E_q=\sum_i A_{qi}I_i=0$ is a source identity, we construct
\begin{equation}
 T=\sum_m r_mM_m+\sum_q\lambda_qE_q,
 \qquad \lambda_q,r_m\in\Q(\boldsymbol x,d).
 \label{eq:reverse}
\end{equation}
Expanding the right-hand side produces an expression in indexed integrals while the added zero identities leave its master coefficients unchanged. We record the identities, their seeds and operators, and the operations used in the construction. Appendix~\ref{app:construction} proves the relation and explains how to recover a reduction path from this record.

This procedure usually produces a sum of integrals. It produces a single indexed integral only when the expanded expression has that form. It also supplies one sufficient seed set and one valid reduction path; it does not establish that either has minimum cost. The labels therefore teach the original models to reproduce a checked construction, and later timing experiments determine whether their choices help Kira.

\subsection{Coverage and separation of training and test data}
Table~\ref{tab:data} summarizes the original collection. The 17,100 family instances contain 57,979 master entries, counted separately in each family, and 2,301,272 targets. Many instances are parameter variations of the same graph. We call a specified assignment of masses and momentum invariants a \emph{parameter profile}. In particular, the higher-loop additions use the restricted vacuum family derived in Appendix~\ref{app:vacuum}; they do not represent general scattering graphs through twelve loops. The test set contains 148,494 single integrals and 541,888 integral expressions. Appendices~\ref{app:families} and \ref{app:basis} specify the families and the checks used to establish their master bases.

Related mass assignments remain in the same training or test group at every loop order. \subsection{What the timing comparisons measure}
Within each comparison, every method receives the same targets and prescribed master basis and uses the same allocated resources. We use \emph{complete reduction time} for the measured time that includes input preparation, model prediction, algebra, checks, and all fallback attempts. Offline training and the construction of reference answers are excluded. The original local-model comparisons repeat each calculation five times. The final complete-cost comparisons use three repetitions, with the method order rotated. Each comparison reports batch medians and states its repetition count.

The baseline also needs to be specified. Most native comparisons use the same modified Kira 3.1 executable for learned and native choices, so improvements shared by that executable do not count as a benefit of the model. The cumulative vacuum comparison instead uses the saved original executable as its baseline. Later sections also distinguish the sum of batch median times from a geometric mean that gives each graph equal weight. The latter averages logarithms of time ratios and then exponentiates the result. Appendix~\ref{app:timing} defines these measures and their descriptive uncertainty intervals. Keeping these conditions explicit allows each speedup to be interpreted against the calculation it actually measures.

\section{Learning the three reduction decisions}
\label{sec:local-models}
We first test the three decisions separately. For each model, we describe the information it receives, the reference labels used in training, and its effect on exact reduction time. This separates the ability to reproduce a recorded choice from the ability to accelerate Kira.

\subsection{Model 1: selecting seeds}
\label{sec:seeds}
The seed network receives the terms of the target, its family, the master indices, and a finite list of candidate seeds. It maps each target term to 32 learned numerical features and combines these with ordered pairs of terms and information about the family and masters. A seed-dependent attention operation then identifies which target terms are most relevant to that candidate. The shared representations and attention follow Refs.~\cite{DeepSets,AttentionIsAllYouNeed}; Appendix~\ref{app:models} gives the full architecture.

The network has 28,097 fitted parameters and assigns a separate score to every candidate:
\begin{equation}
 p_b=\frac{1}{1+e^{-g_\theta(T,F,\mathcal M,\boldsymbol\sigma_b)}},
 \qquad
 \widehat\Sset(T)=\{\boldsymbol\sigma_b\in\Cset:p_b\geq\tfrac12\}.
 \label{eq:seed}
\end{equation}
Here $F$ denotes the family, $\mathcal M$ the master list, $\Cset$ the candidate list, and $g_\theta$ the output of the network with fitted parameters $\theta$. The score estimates whether a seed belongs to the recorded sufficient set. Because a target can require several seeds, several scores can exceed the selection threshold and their sum need not be one. Training uses binary cross-entropy and AdamW~\cite{AdamW}. The network receives neither the target's final master coefficients nor its later reduction steps.

On the 690,382 reserved targets, the model has precision 95.750\%, recall 96.892\%, and $F_1$ score 96.318\%. Its complete predicted set matches the recorded set for 84.332\% of targets. Kira reduces 652,517 targets with the predicted sets, corresponding to 94.515\% sufficiency, and reduces all targets when the full equation pool is available. These percentages differ because matching the recorded set and supplying enough equations are different tests: another seed set can also be sufficient. Appendix~\ref{app:timing} defines the metrics. We report precision and recall because the large number of negative labels can make overall classification accuracy misleading~\cite{SaitoRehmsmeier2015}.

To determine whether this prediction saves time, we use a separate comparison on existing identity pools from twelve reserved one-loop box instances. The weights remain fixed, and the timing test uses threshold $0.01$, chosen before that comparison; the earlier label and sufficiency tests use $1/2$. Both the learned selector and a distance-based rule receive the same basis check and full-pool fallback. For single targets, the learned-to-full-pool time ratio is $0.914$, with descriptive 95\% interval $[0.871,0.958]$, giving 8.6\% less time. Relative to the distance rule, the ratio is $0.913$ with interval $[0.855,0.972]$, giving 8.7\% less time. Every method completes all five repetitions of these twelve cases.

The gain is less clear for larger requests. For sixteen-target batches, the ratio is $0.952$ with interval $[0.772,1.148]$, which includes equal time. Across the full planned seed comparison, 345 trials finish exactly, three remain incomplete, and twelve repetitions are skipped; the timing summary uses only complete paired cases. The identity pools already exist when these timers start. A separate native test that includes IBP generation gives ratio $1.096$ with interval $[0.963,1.275]$. Thus the original network demonstrates a saving for the stated supplied-equation single-target test, while native generation requires a different assessment. Section~\ref{sec:adaptation} reports new seed trees trained directly on scattering reductions.

\subsection{Model 2: selecting elimination rows}
\label{sec:rows}
After the equations have been generated, several allowed source rows may eliminate the next integral. The row network scores these alternatives using 132 inputs that describe the pivot integral, nonzero entries, family, matrix size, and sampled cancellations. It maps each action to 32 learned features, combines information across the available actions, and passes the result through a hidden layer with 64 components. A softmax converts the final scores into probabilities that sum to one, and the network selects the action with the largest probability. The 10,529 fitted parameters determine the choice; exact arithmetic performs the row operation. Appendices~\ref{app:ibp} and \ref{app:models} give the operation and architecture.

The original training examples follow one changing target row while the source identities stay fixed. On 2,558,472 reserved states with a choice, the network selects the recorded action in 95.845\% of cases, compared with 99.473\% for a local deterministic rule. Complete predicted paths agree with the saved master row at three finite-field points for 690,302 of 690,382 targets. These sampled checks support agreement at those points but do not prove symbolic equality. Including prediction, the network takes 17.657 times as long as the local rule.

The more relevant application places the fixed network inside Kira's changing symbolic matrix and preserves Kira's allowed integral order. Across 84 exactly checked trials, this implementation takes 1.305 times the time of stock Kira. We therefore also test smaller policies trained on measured algebraic cost, with compiled prediction and shared features. The latest row-only comparison gives ratio $0.982$ with interval $[0.959,1.003]$, while combining it with the seed policy gives $1.100$ with interval $[0.966,1.279]$ against native choices.

Figure~\ref{fig:row} shows that the later intervals still include equal time. We therefore retain Kira's row choices in the final method. This result explains why the remaining studies focus on decisions that can avoid equation generation or repeated work: reproducing a plausible elimination path did not, by itself, provide a reliable reduction in complete time.

\subsection{Model 3: selecting individual equations}
\label{sec:equations}
Equation selection offers an earlier opportunity to avoid work. The model scores each seed--operator pair before Kira constructs its symbolic coefficients. Its seventeen inputs describe the family, target list, seed indices, and operator; they include the number of loops and targets, extra propagator powers, numerator rank, and distance between seed and target indices. A classification tree~\cite{CART1984} applies feature-threshold decisions and returns a score from its final leaf. The score estimates whether the equation belongs to the useful part of a recorded native reduction. No equation coefficients are needed for prediction, and Kira retains its native treatment of symmetries and masters.

The original fit uses 153,124 candidate equations, including 12,270 positive labels, from fourteen batches in eight parameter profiles. The four graph structures are the two-loop sunrise, planar double box, three-loop vacuum, and three-loop ladder. Validation compares trees of depth eight and twelve, containing 295 and 1,335 nodes, at threshold $0.01$. It selects the deeper tree for the double box and the shallower tree for the vacuum and ladder; the 21-target sunrise case retains the preceding equation policy. A filter is enabled only when validation shows lower complete and preparation times without fallback. Adding learned seed or row choices fails this condition, so native seed and row choices remain active in this comparison.

Testing uses eight new parameter profiles and fourteen target batches. The masses and momentum invariants take rational values, while $d$ remains symbolic, so the coefficient field is $\Q(d)$. Five repetitions of four methods give 280 complete calculations. Native choices, the early equation model, and the distance-based control share the same executable improvements; a fourth method uses the preceding release. This design distinguishes the effect of equation selection from improvements in the surrounding implementation.

Relative to native choices in that executable, the model reduces complete time by 5.7\% and equation-preparation time by 36.2\%. The corresponding ratios are $0.943$ with interval $[0.915,0.970]$ and $0.638$ with interval $[0.546,0.734]$. Preparation includes modular generation, selection, modular solving, and symbolic equation regeneration. The smaller change in complete time reflects the work that remains after preparation. All 280 calculations succeed on their first attempt, and independent exact checks cover 94,970 coefficient comparisons and eight applications supplied only with the prescribed basis.

Appendix~\ref{app:families} defines their propagators and invariants, using the massless limit of the nonplanar family in Ref.~\cite{KiraOriginal}. Four batches use rational kinematics and symbolic $d$. Three more keep the independent invariants symbolic, with coefficient fields $\Q(d,s,t)$, $\Q(d,s_{12},s_{23},s_{34},s_{45},s_{51})$, and $\Q(d,s,t,v)$. Here $s,t$ are four-point invariants, $s_{i,i+1}$ are adjacent five-point invariants, and $v$ is an external squared momentum.

The seven batches contain 88 targets and produce seventy comparison calculations. All final results pass exact checks, but every one of the 35 filtered calculations needs native fallback. The average time ratio is $1.252$, or 25.2\% more time. Checks cover 5,600 coefficient pairs, agreement with enlarged seed regions in all seven cases, and agreement after new rational substitutions in the three symbolic cases. The fallback therefore restores a correct answer at an additional cost. Figure~\ref{fig:transfer} records this transfer test before the new training described next.

\section{Selecting a complete calculation before equation generation}

The local-model tests show that selecting useful equations does not by itself ensure a faster complete reduction.
