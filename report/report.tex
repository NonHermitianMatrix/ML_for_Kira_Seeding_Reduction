\title{Learned selection for exact Feynman integral reduction with Kira}
\author{Rushil Saraswat}
\date{October 14, 2026}
\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
We study learned choices that reduce the time needed for exact Feynman integral reduction with Kira. The measurements include prediction, algebra, checks, and retries. Seed and equation selection save time on their stated inputs; learned row selection gives no reliable saving and is omitted. We train models to choose complete reduction strategies and seed limits for seven known graph structures at one through three loops. Additional ordering predictors give no accepted gain. In the final study, a policy chooses one, two, or four Kira workers. It gives 4.2\% less time in the sum of median full times than the preceding one-worker route and 28.9\% less time than native one-worker choices on the reserved workload. All methods receive four allocated cores, while the selected route uses at most two workers. Its measured CPU use is 18.9\% higher than the preceding route. Giving each graph equal weight yields a time ratio of $0.9817$ against the preceding route. All 900 timed reductions and 50 further applications pass exact checks. Tests use new rational kinematics and symbolic dimension, with known graphs and target patterns. Separate tests give a 48.3\% saving from sharing compatible requests and a 543.8-fold saving from exact recurrences and reuse in a restricted vacuum family through twelve loops. These results use different inputs and controls and do not define a product of speedups.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Higher-order perturbative corrections to scattering amplitudes and particle decay amplitudes contain integrals over loop momenta. Higher loop orders produce many integrals with related numerators and denominators, and algebraic identities can express them in terms of a smaller set of master integrals~\cite{Weinzierl2022,ChetyrkinTkachov}.

Kira performs this reduction by generating integration-by-parts (IBP) identities and solving the resulting linear equations~\cite{Laporta,KiraOriginal}. It returns the coefficient of each master integral in a requested target. Evaluating the masters is a separate calculation. Kira already uses methods that reduce unnecessary equations and algebraic work~\cite{Kira3}. Our aim is to test whether learned models can further reduce that work.

We study three choices: which seeds generate equations, which row eliminates an integral, and which individual equations enter the calculation. We select model types by complete reduction time, including inference and exact checks. Earlier work has also used machine-assisted search to find seed-selection rules~\cite{Song2025}.

We first define the notation used in this paper and explain how the three choices enter Kira. We then describe the data, each model and its tests. We use these results
to study complete-cost model selection and shared reductions for compatible
requests. We then test separate policies for each graph with new seed strategies and selectively expand seed limits to reduce the remaining cost. We then test integral orderings and select the number of Kira workers from the requested targets. We compare full calculation times because fewer equations or accurate predictions alone do not show that a reduction is faster. The appendices give the integral definitions, derivations and detailed measurement rules.

\section{How Kira reduces Feynman integrals}
\label{sec:reduction}
An integral family fixes the propagators and external scalar products. Changing the integer powers gives different integrals in that family~\cite{Weinzierl2022,KiraOriginal}:
\begin{equation}
 I_{\boldsymbol a}(\boldsymbol x,d)
 =\int\prod_{h=1}^{L}\frac{d^d\ell_h}{i\pi^{d/2}}
       \prod_{i=1}^{N}D_i^{-a_i},
 \qquad D_i=q_i^2-m_i^2+i0.
 \label{eq:family}
\end{equation}
Here $L$ is the number of loops, $\ell_h$ is loop momentum $h$, and $N$ is the number of inverse propagators, including auxiliary entries. The momentum $q_i$ is a linear combination of loop and external momenta, and $m_i$ is a particle mass. The symbol $i0$ gives the Feynman prescription. We use dimensional regularization with $d=4-2\epsilon$, where $\epsilon$ is the regulator. The vector $\boldsymbol x$ contains the independent momentum invariants and squared masses.

The integer $a_i$ specifies the power of $D_i$: a positive value gives a denominator, zero removes the factor, and a negative value gives a numerator. Auxiliary inverse propagators provide scalar products that the physical propagators do not cover. A \emph{sector} groups integrals with the same set of positive indices~\cite{KiraOriginal}.

To obtain an IBP equation, choose an index vector $\boldsymbol\sigma$, called a \emph{seed}, and a loop or external momentum $v$. Differentiate with respect to loop momentum $\ell_k$ and integrate. In dimensional regularization, the total derivative gives~\cite{ChetyrkinTkachov}
\begin{equation}
 0=\int\prod_h\frac{d^d\ell_h}{i\pi^{d/2}}
 \frac{\partial}{\partial\ell_k^\mu}
 \left(v^\mu\prod_iD_i^{-\sigma_i}\right)
 =\sum_j A_{\boldsymbol\sigma,o;j}(\boldsymbol x,d)
     I_{\boldsymbol a^{(j)}}.
 \label{eq:ibp}
\end{equation}
The operator label $o$ specifies $k$ and $v$, and $j$ labels the integrals produced by the derivative. Each $A_{\boldsymbol\sigma,o;j}$ is an algebraic coefficient. Appendix~\ref{app:ibp} expands the derivative and shows how an equation eliminates an integral. Several operators can act on one seed, so one seed generally produces several equations.

Kira generates equations within specified index limits, uses symmetries and removes redundant equations. It then eliminates integrals in order and substitutes the resulting relations back into the targets~\cite{KiraOriginal,Kira3}. Kira can use exact arithmetic modulo a prime to select equations or reconstruct rational functions. For a prime $p$, residues modulo $p$ form a finite field. Kira can also reduce symbolic rational functions directly~\cite{KiraOriginal,Kira2}. A target can be one integral or a sum $T=\sum_u c_u I_{\boldsymbol a^{(u)}}$. Its reduction is
\begin{equation}
 T=\sum_{m=1}^{n_M}r_{Tm}(\boldsymbol x,d)M_m,
 \qquad r_{Tm}\in\Q(\boldsymbol x,d).
 \label{eq:master}
\end{equation}
Here $c_u$ is a target coefficient, $M_m$ is master integral $m$, and $n_M$ is the number of masters. The notation $\Q(\boldsymbol x,d)$ means rational functions of the invariants, squared masses and dimension with rational-number coefficients. We check that every target reduces to the prescribed master basis, then compare its coefficients with an independently obtained exact reduction.

Each equation is one row of the coefficient matrix, and each integral is one column. Elimination removes an integral by combining rows. Native Kira uses its built-in selection rules. Table~\ref{tab:decisions} places the three models in this procedure. Each model can save different work, and each prediction has a cost. Exact algebra still determines the coefficients. If the selected equations cannot reduce a target to the prescribed basis, the calculation retries with native Kira choices. We call this retry a \emph{fallback}.

\section{Training data and fair comparisons}
\label{sec:data}
To train the original models, we construct targets from checked master relations and exact source identities. Let $E_q=\sum_i A_{qi}I_i=0$ be identity $q$. We form
\begin{equation}
 T=\sum_m r_mM_m+\sum_q\lambda_qE_q,
 \qquad \lambda_q,r_m\in\Q(\boldsymbol x,d).
 \label{eq:reverse}
\end{equation}
Expanding the right-hand side gives a target whose master coefficients are known. We save the seeds, operators and construction steps. Appendix~\ref{app:construction} proves the relation. The target can be a sum of integrals; it is a single indexed integral only when the expansion has that form. The saved path gives one sufficient reduction without proving minimum cost.

Table~\ref{tab:data} gives the original collection size. It contains 57,979 master entries counted separately in each family. It covers loop orders one through twelve, but its higher-loop additions belong to the restricted vacuum family in Appendix~\ref{app:vacuum}. Many family instances differ only in their parameters. These counts therefore do not describe 17,100 different scattering graphs. The test set contains 148,494 single integrals and 541,888 integral expressions. Appendix~\ref{app:families} defines the families, and Appendix~\ref{app:basis} explains the master-basis checks.

Related mass profiles stay together in either training or testing at all loop orders. The seed and row networks each receive twenty further training passes over the expanded training set. They retain their earlier fitted parameters and optimizer state. Some older test profiles had appeared in earlier evaluations, so this test concerns reserved parameter groups rather than entirely new graphs. Later native tests use separate training, validation and test cases. Validation chooses the settings; test data do not change them.

Each time comparison uses the same targets, master basis and resources for all methods. Full time includes preparation, prediction, algebra, checks and every fallback. Offline training and reference discovery remain outside this time. The main comparisons use five repetitions and compare median times. Most native tests use the same modified Kira 3.1 executable for learned and native choices. The cumulative test uses the saved original executable as its baseline. Appendix~\ref{app:timing} defines the averages and uncertainty intervals.

\section{Model 1: seed selection}
\label{sec:seeds}
The original seed network receives the target terms, family, master indices and a finite candidate seed list. It converts each term into 32 learned numerical features. It then combines these features, ordered pairs of terms, family information and master information to score each seed. The shared term representation and attention operation follow Refs.~\cite{DeepSets,AttentionIsAllYouNeed}. Appendix~\ref{app:models} gives the architecture. The network has 28,097 fitted parameters and assigns each seed a score:
\begin{equation}
 p_b=\frac{1}{1+e^{-g_\theta(T,F,\mathcal M,\boldsymbol\sigma_b)}},
 \qquad
 \widehat\Sset(T)=\{\boldsymbol\sigma_b\in\Cset:p_b\geq\tfrac12\}.
 \label{eq:seed}
\end{equation}
Here $F$ is the family, $\mathcal M$ is the master list, $\Cset$ is the candidate list, and $g_\theta$ is the network output with fitted parameters $\theta$. The score estimates whether a seed belongs to the saved sufficient set. Several seeds can be selected, so their scores do not need to sum to one. Training uses binary cross-entropy and AdamW~\cite{AdamW}; Appendix~\ref{app:models} derives the loss. Prediction receives neither the final master coefficients nor later reduction steps.

On the 690,382 test targets, precision is 95.750\%, recall is 96.892\%, and $F_1$ is 96.318\%. Complete seed sets match the saved sets for 84.332\% of targets. Kira completes 652,517 reductions with the predicted sets, giving 94.515\% sufficiency; it completes all targets with the full pool. These measures answer different questions because a different seed set can still reduce a target. Appendix~\ref{app:timing} defines the metrics. We report precision and recall because the many zero labels can make overall accuracy misleading~\cite{SaitoRehmsmeier2015}.

The separate time test uses existing identity pools from twelve reserved one-loop box instances. It uses unchanged weights and threshold $0.01$, fixed before the comparison. The earlier label and sufficiency tests use threshold $1/2$. Both the learned selector and a distance-based rule receive the same basis check and full-pool fallback. For single targets, the learned/full-pool time ratio is $0.914$, with a descriptive 95\% interval of $[0.871,0.958]$. This means 8.6\% less time. The ratio to the distance rule is $0.913$ with interval $[0.855,0.972]$, or 8.7\% less time. Every method completes all five repetitions for these twelve cases.

For sixteen-target batches, the ratio is $0.952$ with interval $[0.772,1.148]$, so the measurements do not establish a consistent saving. Across the planned seed comparison, 345 trials finish exactly, three are incomplete, and twelve repetitions are skipped. The timing summary uses complete paired cases. This test excludes the original generation of the identity pool. A separate native test that includes IBP generation gives $1.096$ with interval $[0.963,1.275]$. The original seed model therefore has a demonstrated saving only in the stated supplied-equation test. Section~\ref{sec:adaptation} studies new seed trees trained directly on scattering reductions.

\section{Model 2: row selection}
\label{sec:rows}
The row network receives the current matrix and the permitted source rows for the next elimination. Its 132 inputs describe the pivot integral, nonzero row entries, family, matrix size and sampled cancellations. It gives each action 32 learned features, combines information across actions, and uses a 64-component hidden layer to produce a score. A softmax normalizes the exponentials of the scores into probabilities, and the model chooses the largest. The network has 10,529 parameters. Exact arithmetic performs the chosen operation. Appendix~\ref{app:ibp} derives that operation, and Appendix~\ref{app:models} defines the network.

The original training data follow one changing target row with fixed source identities. On 2,558,472 reserved states with a choice, the network selects the saved action in 95.845\% of states. A local deterministic rule reaches 99.473\%. Complete paths match the saved master row at three finite-field points for 690,302 of 690,382 targets. These sampled checks do not prove symbolic equality. Including prediction, the network takes 17.657 times the local rule's time.

We also use the fixed network inside Kira's changing symbolic matrix while preserving the allowed integral order. Across 84 exactly checked trials, full time is 1.305 times stock Kira time. Later, smaller policies learn from measured algebraic cost and use compiled prediction and shared features. Their latest row-only ratio is $0.982$ with interval $[0.959,1.003]$. Adding the seed policy gives $1.100$ with interval $[0.966,1.279]$ against native choices.

These intervals do not establish a reliable saving, as Figure~\ref{fig:row} shows. We therefore omit learned row selection from the retained method and let Kira choose the rows. We preserve these negative results because agreement with a saved reduction path did not produce faster algebra.

\section{Model 3: equation selection}
\label{sec:equations}
The equation model scores each seed--operator pair before Kira constructs its symbolic coefficients. It uses seventeen features that describe the family, targets, seed indices and operator. They include the loop count, target count, extra propagator powers, numerator rank and distance between seed and target indices. It needs no equation coefficients. A classification tree~\cite{CART1984} estimates whether the equation belongs to the useful part of a recorded native reduction. The tree follows feature-threshold decisions to a leaf, which stores the score. Kira keeps its own treatment of symmetries and masters.

The original fit uses 153,124 candidate equations, including 12,270 positive labels, from fourteen batches in eight parameter profiles. The graphs are a two-loop sunrise, planar double box, three-loop vacuum and three-loop ladder. Validation chooses between trees of depth eight and twelve, with 295 and 1,335 nodes, at threshold $0.01$. The double box uses the deeper tree; the vacuum and ladder use the shallower tree; the 21-target sunrise case retains the preceding equation policy. Validation requires lower full and preparation times with no fallback before activating a filter. Adding learned seed or row choices fails these criteria, so this test keeps native seed and row choices.

The test uses eight new parameter profiles and fourteen target batches. Masses and momentum invariants have rational values while $d$ remains symbolic, so the coefficients lie in $\Q(d)$. Five repetitions of four methods give 280 full calculations. Native choices, the early equation model and the distance-based control use the same executable improvements. A fourth method uses the preceding release. This comparison separates the model's choices from improvements shared by all methods.

The equation model reduces full time by 5.7\% and equation-preparation time by 36.2\%. Their ratios are $0.943$ with interval $[0.915,0.970]$ and $0.638$ with interval $[0.546,0.734]$. Preparation includes modular generation, selection, modular solving and symbolic equation regeneration. Other stages limit the full-time saving. All 280 calculations finish on their first attempt. Independent exact checks cover 94,970 coefficient comparisons and eight applications given only the prescribed basis.

We next apply the fixed depth-twelve tree to a massless nonplanar two-loop box, massless pentagon and box with one external virtuality. These structures were absent from its training data. Appendix~\ref{app:families} gives their propagators and invariants. The nonplanar family follows the massless limit in Ref.~\cite{KiraOriginal}. Four batches use rational kinematics and symbolic $d$. Three further batches keep all independent invariants symbolic, giving coefficient fields $\Q(d,s,t)$, $\Q(d,s_{12},s_{23},s_{34},s_{45},s_{51})$ and $\Q(d,s,t,v)$. Here $s,t$ are four-point invariants, $s_{i,i+1}$ are adjacent five-point invariants, and $v$ is an external squared momentum.

The seven batches contain 88 targets and give seventy comparison calculations. All results pass exact checks, but all 35 filtered calculations require native fallback. Their average time ratio is $1.252$, which means 25.2\% more time. Checks cover 5,600 coefficient pairs. Enlarged seed regions agree in all seven cases, and new rational substitutions agree for the three symbolic cases. This establishes correct completion through fallback. It also shows that the original filter does not save time on these inputs. Figure~\ref{fig:transfer} preserves this result from before the new training.

\section{Overall results}
\label{sec:overall}
The original comparisons support learned seeds for the tested supplied-equation requests and learned equations for the tested native families. They give no reliable saving from learned row selection. Table~\ref{tab:overall} separates these results because they use different inputs and time different amounts of work. Multiplying the speedups would not give a measured combined speedup. The original native equation experiment also gives no established gain from activating both selectors together. Section~\ref{sec:adaptation} reports the separate scattering-training study.
Section~\ref{sec:workflow} then tests complete-cost selection and joint
requests. Its 48.3\% saving comes mainly from sharing the calculation;
the learned cost model adds no reliable saving over the integrated native
control. This result is a direct comparison of identical paired requests.
The separate graph-specific study in Section~\ref{sec:type-specialists}
gives 6.3\% less time relative to native choices and ratio $0.9441$
to the fixed strategy per graph. Its gains cannot be multiplied by the
joint-request saving because the two studies use different requests.
Section~\ref{sec:basis-completion} then measures 8.8\% less time relative to the preceding route and 14.8\% less time relative to native choices on new profiles.
The ordering study in Section~\ref{sec:ordering-policy} gives 1.4\% more time against that preceding method and 15.0\% less time against native choices on another reserved set.

For the restricted vacuum family, exact recurrences express the coefficients through $z=L(d-2)$. This lets one calculation serve several loop orders and targets. The final method reuses intermediate coefficients, applies exact arithmetic and checks each result with an independent Taylor expansion. Appendix~\ref{app:vacuum} derives both calculations. Kira remains available if the direct check fails. The measured gain includes these mathematical changes and program improvements; it does not isolate a machine learning effect.

Eight fresh groups each contain three mass profiles, with two, three or four massive denominators, and request loop orders one through twelve. Five repetitions compare the saved original Kira 3.1 executable, a later full-pool control and the recurrence method on identical requests. All 120 calculations pass exact checks. The final/original ratio is $0.001839$, giving a speedup of $543.815$ with interval $[488.134,605.064]$. A separate check verifies 27,960 nonzero target--master coefficient polynomials. The final method needs no Kira calls on these successful requests; each baseline method makes 1,440 calls across its trials.

Earlier checks at loop orders 13, 16, 24 and 32 use the same restricted family, and the recurrence proof permits any positive integer loop order. General high-loop scattering remains outside this result.
\section{Conclusion}
Learned seed and equation selection reduce full time in their stated test
domains. We omit learned row selection because its complete-time tests
give no reliable saving. The later models select a complete reduction
strategy before equation generation, using measured cost and exact
fallback. This lets the model avoid expensive algebra before it starts.
The ordering comparison adds no accepted predictive gain, so it does
not replace the preceding seed strategy.

The final worker-count study changes summed median time from 82.21 to 78.73 seconds on 36 reserved batches, giving 4.2\% less time against the preceding one-worker route. Native one-worker choices take 110.67 seconds. All methods receive four allocated cores, while the selected route uses at most two workers. CPU use rises by 18.9\%. The graph-weighted ratio to the preceding method is $0.9817$, and the comparison with fixed counts is reported separately. All 900 timed reductions and 50 further applications pass exact checks. The selector uses one decision per batch, while Kira retains exact algebra.

Sharing compatible requests gives a separate 48.3\% saving. Exact
recurrences and reuse give a 543.8-fold saving in the restricted vacuum
family through twelve loops. These comparisons use different inputs and
controls, so their speedups cannot be multiplied.

The final native tests cover seven known graph structures at one through
three loops. They retain the training target patterns and use new rational
kinematics with symbolic dimension. The report retains slower cases,
retry costs, and CPU use. These results establish the measured choices
within this domain. General high-loop scattering and globally optimal
reduction paths remain outside the demonstrated results.
