\author{Rushil Saraswat}

\begin{abstract}
We study three opportunities for lightweight machine learning to reduce the cost of integration-by-parts reduction: seed selection, row selection during elimination, and target-dependent equation selection. Exact algebra determines the reduction coefficients, and incomplete selected systems trigger a native fallback. A seed classifier trained on constructed integral relations gives sufficient equation sets for 94.515\% of 690,382 test targets. In a separate supplied-equation comparison, seed selection reduces single-target calculation time by 8.6\%. Learned row selection gives no reliable improvement in complete native reductions, so we omit it from the retained approach and keep seed and equation selection for their separately validated domains. Selecting equations before coefficient construction reduces preparation time by 36.2\% and complete native time by 5.7\% on new parameter profiles within four tested graph structures. A frozen filter increases time by 25.2\% on new scattering families, including symbolic kinematics, which limits this result's domain. Separately, exact recurrences and reuse give a 543.8-fold speedup for a restricted vacuum family; this is a combined mathematical and implementation gain. The measurements support selective use of learned decisions and retain native choices where their complete cost is lower.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Higher-order perturbative corrections to scattering amplitudes and particle decay amplitudes contain loop-momentum integrals. Their number and algebraic complexity make reduction to master integrals an important part of precision calculations in quantum field theory~\cite{Weinzierl2022}. Integration-by-parts (IBP) identities relate integrals with different propagator powers~\cite{ChetyrkinTkachov}. Kira implements the Laporta approach, organizes these relations into linear systems, selects equations and expresses requested integrals through a master basis~\cite{Laporta,KiraOriginal}. This reduction supplies rational coefficients; evaluating the master integrals remains a separate problem.

Kira already uses optimized seeding and equation selection, so a learned decision must improve upon an existing reduction strategy~\cite{Kira3}. Earlier work has used machine-assisted search to discover seed-priority functions~\cite{Song2025}. Here we study inexpensive models that guide three decisions during a reduction: which seeds supply identities, which allowed source row eliminates the next integral, and which identities need coefficient construction for the requested targets. We call these System 1 models to specify their role as fast decision rules; the term imposes no particular neural architecture.

Our criterion is complete calculation time, including model evaluation and failed attempts. We first define the reduction and the three decision points, then describe each model and its measured results. We retain the seed and equation models in the domains supported by their separate comparisons and omit learned row selection. The combined-results section states the limits of this choice and distinguishes learned gains from exact mathematical reuse. Appendices give the derivations, model details and measurement protocol needed to assess the results.

\section{Kira reduction and the three decision points}
\label{sec:reduction}
We first define the notation used in this paper. An integral family fixes inverse propagators and the external scalar products, while its index vector specifies their powers~\cite{Weinzierl2022,KiraOriginal}:
\begin{equation}
 I_{\boldsymbol a}(\boldsymbol x,d)
 =\int\prod_{h=1}^{L}\frac{d^d\ell_h}{i\pi^{d/2}}
       \prod_{i=1}^{N}D_i^{-a_i},
 \qquad D_i=q_i^2-m_i^2+i0.
 \label{eq:family}
\end{equation}
Here $L$ is the number of loops, $\ell_h$ is a loop momentum, $d=4-2\epsilon$ is the regulated dimension, and $\epsilon$ is the dimensional regulator. The vector $q_i$ is a linear combination of loop and external momenta, $m_i$ is a mass, and $i0$ specifies the Feynman prescription. The vector $\boldsymbol x$ contains independent momentum invariants and squared masses. The integer $a_i$ is the power of denominator $i$: a positive value places it in the denominator, zero removes it, and a negative value gives a numerator factor. Auxiliary inverse propagators complete the independent scalar products when necessary. A sector specifies which indices are positive~\cite{KiraOriginal}.

A seed is an index vector $\boldsymbol\sigma$ at which one applies a total derivative. An IBP operator differentiates with respect to a loop momentum and contracts with a loop or external momentum. Under dimensional regularization, the integrated total derivative gives a relation~\cite{ChetyrkinTkachov}
\begin{equation}
 0=\int\prod_h\frac{d^d\ell_h}{i\pi^{d/2}}
 \frac{\partial}{\partial\ell_k^\mu}
 \left(v^\mu\prod_iD_i^{-\sigma_i}\right)
 =\sum_j A_{\boldsymbol\sigma,o;j}(\boldsymbol x,d)
     I_{\boldsymbol a^{(j)}}.
 \label{eq:ibp}
\end{equation}
The label $o$ identifies the derivative and vector $v$, $j$ labels the integrals produced, and $A_{\boldsymbol\sigma,o;j}$ is their coefficient. Appendix~\ref{app:ibp} expands this identity and explains how a nonzero pivot eliminates an integral. A seed generally supplies several equations, so selecting seeds and selecting individual equations are different decisions.

Kira generates relations in a bounded index region, applies symmetry and equation-selection procedures, and performs forward elimination and back substitution~\cite{KiraOriginal,Kira3}. Finite-field calculations can identify useful equations or support rational reconstruction, while symbolic reduction manipulates rational functions directly~\cite{KiraOriginal,Kira2}. For a target expression $T=\sum_u c_u I_{\boldsymbol a^{(u)}}$, the desired result is
\begin{equation}
 T=\sum_{m=1}^{n_M}r_{Tm}(\boldsymbol x,d)M_m,
 \qquad r_{Tm}\in\Q(\boldsymbol x,d).
 \label{eq:master}
\end{equation}
Here $c_u$ specifies the requested expression, $n_M$ is the number of prescribed masters, and $M_m$ denotes master $m$. We check completion against that prescribed basis. Agreement with a separately obtained exact reduction tests the coefficients; it does not establish that every chosen seed is necessary.

The first intervention reduces the seed set before equation generation. The second selects a source row among permitted elimination actions while preserving Kira's integral order and protected masters. The third selects seed--operator pairs before constructing their symbolic coefficients. Each choice can save different work and introduces its own prediction cost. We therefore test complete reductions as well as the affected stage.

\section{Integral data and comparison design}
\label{sec:data}
We constructed targets from checked master relations and exact source identities, retaining the seeds, operators, coefficients and reverse construction steps. If the identity rows are $E_q=\sum_i A_{qi}I_i=0$, the construction has the form
\begin{equation}
 T=\sum_m r_mM_m+\sum_q\lambda_qE_q,
 \qquad \lambda_q,r_m\in\Q(\boldsymbol x,d).
 \label{eq:reverse}
\end{equation}
Expanding the right-hand side produces the stored target expression and its known master expansion. Appendix~\ref{app:construction} gives the algebraic check. Such a construction can produce a sum of integrals; a single indexed target requires the expanded nonmaster terms to have that form. The construction records a sufficient path without proving minimum cost.

The resulting collection contains 17,100 family instances, 57,979 family-local master entries and 2,301,272 targets. The grouped division contains 1,610,890 training targets in 11,963 families and 690,382 test targets in 5,137 families. Related mass profiles stay in one split across loop orders. The collection spans $L=1,\ldots,12$, but many instances are parameter variations of a small set of graph structures. In particular, the added higher-loop data use the restricted vacuum family defined in Appendix~\ref{app:vacuum}. The test set contains 148,494 single integrals and 541,888 integral expressions. These counts describe our constructed dataset; they do not count distinct scattering topologies.

The original seed and row models each received twenty additional training passes over the expanded training data, retaining the earlier model and optimizer states. Test families remained outside parameter updates. Earlier stages had evaluated some of the older reserved profiles, so the expanded test is a grouped parameter-transfer test rather than a wholly new topology test. Later native comparisons use separate development, validation and reserved physical profiles, with policies fixed before the final timing comparisons.

Every runtime comparison uses identical requested targets and a common master basis for its competing methods. Complete time includes preparation, inference, exact algebra, result checks and fallback as specified in Appendix~\ref{app:timing}. Most native tests use the same modified Kira 3.1 executable for learned and native choices. The cumulative comparison separately uses the saved original executable. We report five-repeat median ratios and descriptive intervals over physical groups; these quantify the recorded cases and do not establish performance over all possible integral families.

\section{Model 1: selecting seeds}
\label{sec:seeds}
The seed model receives the target terms, the family definition, the prescribed master indices and a finite candidate seed list. It uses shared 32-component term representations, all ordered pairs of distinct terms, family and basis summaries, and a candidate-dependent attention average. Shared aggregation and attention follow the constructions of Refs.~\cite{DeepSets,AttentionIsAllYouNeed}; Appendix~\ref{app:models} specifies our implementation. The model has 28,097 adjustable parameters and assigns each seed its own sigmoid score,
\begin{equation}
 p_b=\frac{1}{1+e^{-g_\theta(T,F,\mathcal M,\boldsymbol\sigma_b)}},
 \qquad
 \widehat\Sset(T)=\{\boldsymbol\sigma_b\in\Cset:p_b\geq\tfrac12\}.
 \label{eq:seed}
\end{equation}
Here $F$ is the family, $\mathcal M$ is the master list, $\Cset$ is the candidate pool and $\theta$ denotes fitted parameters. The score describes membership in a recorded sufficient seed set. Scores for different seeds need not sum to one, and a target can require several selected seeds. Training uses target-averaged binary cross-entropy with AdamW~\cite{AdamW}; Appendix~\ref{app:models} derives the loss from the label likelihood. No target master coefficients or future reduction steps enter prediction.

On all 690,382 test targets, precision is 95.750\%, recall is 96.892\%, and $F_1$ is 96.318\%. Complete predicted sets match the recorded sets for 84.332\% of targets. The equations supplied by the predicted seeds suffice for 652,517 targets, or 94.515\%, in the Kira sufficiency test. The full pool suffices for all targets in that test. These results measure different questions: agreement with one stored set and the ability to complete a reduction. Appendix~\ref{app:timing} defines the metrics. Candidate accuracy alone obscures the frequent zero labels, a known issue for imbalanced classification~\cite{SaitoRehmsmeier2015}.

The separate timing experiment reduces constructed expressions from existing identity pools in twelve held-out one-loop box instances. It uses unchanged weights with threshold $0.01$, fixed before this reserved comparison; the preceding label and sufficiency tests retain threshold $1/2$. Both the learned selector and a distance-based selector receive the same early basis check and full-pool fallback. For one target at a time, the learned/full-pool complete-time ratio is $0.914$ with descriptive 95\% interval $[0.871,0.958]$; the ratio to the matched distance control is $0.913$ with interval $[0.855,0.972]$. Thus the measured reduction is 8.6\% against the full pool and 8.7\% against the distance control. All five repetitions complete for every method in these twelve single-target cases.

For sixteen-target batches, the learned/full-pool ratio is $0.952$ with interval $[0.772,1.148]$, which does not establish a consistent saving. Across the full planned comparison, 345 trials complete exactly, three measured trials are incomplete and twelve repetitions are skipped. The timing summary uses complete paired batches and preserves these failures. This seed result excludes the original construction of the supplied identity pool. In a separate native comparison that includes IBP generation, the later compiled seed route gives a ratio of $1.096$ with interval $[0.963,1.275]$. We retain learned seeds for the supported supplied-equation domain and make no general native speedup claim.

\section{Model 2: choosing elimination rows}
\label{sec:rows}
The row model receives the current coefficient matrix and a finite list of permitted source-row actions. Its 132 features describe the pivot integral, row support, family, matrix size and sampled cancellation counts. A shared neural map produces 32-component action vectors, combines their mean and componentwise maximum, and uses a 64-component hidden layer to assign scores. The network has 10,529 parameters. A softmax converts its scores into a probability distribution over the supplied actions; the highest score selects the next row operation. Appendix~\ref{app:models} defines the distribution and training objective. Exact arithmetic applies the selected operation, as derived in Appendix~\ref{app:ibp}.

Training traces contain one changing target row with fixed source identities. The reserved traces contain 2,558,472 states with more than one action. The model chooses the recorded action in 95.845\% of those states, while the local deterministic rule reaches 99.473\%. Complete paths agree with the recorded master row at three finite-field samples for 690,302 of 690,382 targets. These sample checks do not constitute a proof of symbolic equality. The original model takes 17.657 times the local rule's time, including prediction, in this target-row test.

We then inserted the frozen model into Kira's evolving symbolic matrix, preserving the allowed integral order. In 84 exact-checked trials, the complete-time ratio to stock Kira is $1.305$. The original training states therefore did not yield a useful native row policy. Subsequent smaller policies use measured immediate and continuation costs, compiled inference and shared finite-field features. The latest row-only complete-time ratio is $0.982$ with descriptive interval $[0.959,1.003]$. This interval includes equal time. Adding the row and seed policies together gives $1.100$ with interval $[0.966,1.279]$ against native choices in the same later comparison.

Figure~\ref{fig:row} distinguishes the initial slowdown from the uncertain later result. We omit the second model from the retained reduction method because these complete-time measurements do not establish a reliable gain. Kira continues to choose elimination rows. This decision preserves the exact row-model tests as evidence and avoids treating agreement with a recorded path as evidence of faster algebra.

\section{Model 3: selecting equations before coefficient construction}
\label{sec:equations}
The equation model scores a seed--operator pair for the requested target list before Kira constructs that identity. Its 17 features describe loop and external-momentum counts, target count, seed sector, additional propagator powers, numerator rank, distances from target limits and the operator index. These features require no symbolic equation coefficients. A classification tree estimates membership in the useful support of a recorded native reduction, following the tree framework of Ref.~\cite{CART1984}. Accepted pairs enter Kira's native coefficient construction. Symmetry relations and the prescribed basis retain their native treatment.

We trained trees on 153,124 candidate equations, with 12,270 positive labels, from fourteen batches in eight physical profiles. The structures include a two-loop sunrise, a planar double box, a three-loop vacuum and a three-loop ladder. Candidate trees have maximum depths eight and twelve, with 295 and 1,335 nodes. Validation selects depth twelve for the double box and depth eight for vacuum and ladder cases, at a retention threshold of $0.01$. The recorded 21-target sunrise domain retains the earlier equation policy. Validation requires lower complete and preparation times without fallback before activating an early filter. Additional seed and row policies fail the corresponding composition criteria, so these native equation tests retain native seed and row choices.

The independent comparison uses eight new parameter profiles and fourteen target batches. All masses and momentum invariants take specified rational values while $d$ remains symbolic, so coefficient comparisons occur in $\Q(d)$. Five alternating repetitions of four methods give 280 complete workflows. The native, early-filter and distance-control arms use the same executable and shared runtime improvements; the fourth arm uses the preceding release. This design measures model-dependent choices without assigning shared executable improvements to the model.

The deployed equation policy gives a complete-time ratio of $0.943$ with descriptive 95\% interval $[0.915,0.970]$ against native choices. Its equation-preparation ratio is $0.638$ with interval $[0.546,0.734]$. These correspond to 5.7\% less complete time and 36.2\% less preparation time. Preparation includes modular generation, equation selection and solving, and symbolic equation regeneration. Figure~\ref{fig:equations} shows why a larger stage saving produces a smaller complete saving. All 280 workflows and eight applications with only a prescribed basis pass the independent exact audit, covering 94,970 coefficient comparisons. Every method completes on its first attempt.

We next froze the depth-twelve tree and tested previously unseen scattering structures: a massless nonplanar two-loop box, a massless one-loop pentagon, and a massless box with one independent external virtuality. The nonplanar family follows the massless limit of the corresponding family in Ref.~\cite{KiraOriginal}. Four rational-kinematic batches keep $d$ symbolic; three additional batches keep their independent invariants symbolic. The latter reductions occur in $\Q(d,s,t)$, $\Q(d,s_{12},s_{23},s_{34},s_{45},s_{51})$, and $\Q(d,s,t,v)$, respectively. Here $s,t$ are four-point invariants, $s_{i,i+1}$ are adjacent five-point invariants, and $v$ is the external squared momentum.

The seven batches contain 88 targets and give seventy comparison workflows. Every filtered workflow requires native fallback. The complete-time ratio is $1.252$, or 25.2\% more time. All workflows pass exact comparisons, including 5,600 coefficient-pair checks. Enlarged seed regions agree for all seven cases, and fresh rational specializations agree for the three symbolic cases. These checks support the returned reductions while the measured slowdown prevents activation of the filter in these new domains. Figure~\ref{fig:transfer} reports each case rather than extrapolating the earlier gain.

\section{Overall results and retained method}
\label{sec:overall}
The retained learned methods are seed selection and target-dependent equation selection. We omit learned row selection and keep Kira's native elimination choices. The two retained models operate in their separately validated domains: learned seeds for supported supplied-equation requests, and the equation policy for supported native requests. Their measured gains cannot be multiplied because the comparisons use different inputs and include different work. The native equation experiment does not establish a gain from activating both models together; validation rejected that composition. For an unsupported domain, the application uses native choices or retries them after an incomplete experimental selection.

We separately measured the cumulative effect of exact mathematical reuse and program changes in the restricted vacuum family. Appendix~\ref{app:vacuum} derives the shared variable $z=L(d-2)$, the decreasing-power recurrences and an independent Taylor check. The final implementation evaluates these recurrences with a fixed validated ordering, reuses intermediate coefficients across targets and loop orders, performs compiled exact arithmetic and saves lossless output. Kira remains a fallback if the direct check fails. No additional learned ordering supplies an established gain in this final specialized route.

Eight fresh groups contain three mass profiles each, with two, three or four distinct massive denominators, and request loop orders one through twelve. Five repetitions compare the saved original Kira 3.1 executable, a later full-pool control and the final specialized route on identical requests. All 120 workflows pass exact checks. The measured final/original ratio is $0.001839$, equivalent to a speedup of $543.815$ with descriptive 95\% interval $[488.134,605.064]$. An independent audit checks 27,960 nonzero target--master coefficient polynomials. The final route makes no Kira calls on these successful requests, while each baseline arm makes 1,440 calls across its trials.

The cumulative benchmark generates and supplies Euler and mass partial-fraction identities within the timed workflow. It does not generate a general native high-loop IBP system. Earlier checks at $L=13,16,24,32$ use the same restricted family, whose recurrence proof permits arbitrary positive integer $L$. These results therefore concern a stated mathematical structure rather than general high-loop scattering. The broader symbolic tests in Section~\ref{sec:equations} establish exact completion with native fallback, but give no further learned speedup.
\section{Conclusion}
The measurements show that learned decisions help only when their complete cost is lower than the work they avoid. Seed selection supplies sufficient equations for 94.515\% of the expanded test targets and reduces time by 8.6\% in the separate single-target supplied-equation comparison. Target-dependent equation selection reduces native preparation time by 36.2\% and complete time by 5.7\% within the tested graph structures. Learned row selection gives no reliable complete-time improvement, so we omit that second model and retain the other two methods with their stated domain restrictions.

New scattering structures and symbolic invariants expose a transfer limit: the frozen equation filter requires fallback and increases time by 25.2\%. The retained application therefore uses native choices in those domains. The separate 543.8-fold cumulative speedup comes from exact recurrences, shared reductions and implementation changes in a restricted vacuum family. General native multiloop speedups and a beneficial simultaneous seed--equation composition remain unestablished by these measurements. The exact checks and complete-cost comparisons make those limits part of the result.
