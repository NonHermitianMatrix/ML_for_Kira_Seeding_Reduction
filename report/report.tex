\section{Introduction}
Feynman integrals arise when we integrate over the internal loop momenta in higher-order perturbative corrections to scattering amplitudes and particle decay amplitudes. In quantum chromodynamics, these corrections contribute to predictions for cross sections and decay rates. Many integrals in the same calculation have related numerator and denominator structures, and integration-by-parts (IBP) identities allow us to express them in terms of a smaller set of master integrals~\cite{Weinzierl2022,ChetyrkinTkachov}.

We study how to reduce the time needed for this decomposition into master integrals. Kira generates identities and solves the resulting linear system, with existing seed and equation-selection methods that remove some unnecessary work~\cite{KiraOriginal,Laporta,Kira3}. Exact recurrences can avoid a general linear system for suitable integral structures, while learned choices can reduce the work left for Kira. Throughout this paper, a target is an integral or linear combination requested by the user, and a seed is an index vector at which Kira generates identities. Decomposition determines the coefficients of the master integrals; their numerical or analytic evaluation is a further calculation~\cite{Weinzierl2022}.

Our method combines independent-loop factorization, exact analytic recurrences, and learned seed choices. We also study elimination-row and equation selection to determine where prediction helps. Earlier machine-assisted searches motivate the seed intervention~\cite{Song2025,TubeSeeding2026}, while known analytic one-loop reduction motivates the recurrence stage~\cite{Hu2025}. We measure the complete application time, including model use, exact algebra, checks, and unsuccessful attempts. Separate controls distinguish the benefit of a learned decision from the benefit of exact formulas and fixed tuning.

We first define the notation used in this paper and derive the decomposition problem. We then explain the exact stage and the three learned interventions, compare their measured benefits and limits, and test their integration. We first derive a triangle formula and train a complete-cost model to choose shared native reductions. We then extend exact integration to connected massless bubble trees through thirteen loops and test the unchanged learned choices on fresh two-loop batches. A separate audit records completion and failure across vacuum, propagator, and scattering sequences at each loop count. It also includes symbolic kinematics. The companion notes preserve historical methods, secondary experiments, and their full results.

\section{The reduction problem and the three decisions}
\label{sec:physics}
An integral family specifies inverse propagators and external kinematics. We use the convention~\cite{Weinzierl2022,KiraOriginal}
\begin{equation}
 I_{\boldsymbol a}(\boldsymbol x,d)=
 \int\prod_{h=1}^{L}\frac{d^d\ell_h}{i\pi^{d/2}}
 \prod_{j=1}^{N}D_j^{-a_j},\qquad D_j=q_j^2-m_j^2+i0.
 \label{eq:integral}
\end{equation}
Here $L$ is the number of loops, $\ell_h$ is loop momentum $h$, $q_j$ is a fixed linear combination of loop and external momenta, and $m_j$ is the mass associated with propagator $j$. The vector $\boldsymbol x$ contains squared masses and external momentum invariants. Dimensional regularization uses $d=4-2\epsilon$, where $\epsilon$ is the regulator, and $i0$ specifies the Feynman pole prescription. The normalization of the measure is a convention. A positive integer $a_j$ places $D_j$ in the denominator, a negative integer places it in the numerator, and zero removes that factor. The list of $N$ entries includes auxiliary inverse propagators when the physical denominators alone do not span the loop scalar products~\cite{Weinzierl2022}.

Integrals with the same positive-index positions belong to the same sector. A subsector removes some of those positions~\cite{KiraOriginal}. At seed $\boldsymbol\sigma$, differentiating the integrand with respect to loop momentum $\ell_k$ gives the identity~\cite{ChetyrkinTkachov}
\begin{equation}
 0=\int\prod_h\frac{d^d\ell_h}{i\pi^{d/2}}
 \frac{\partial}{\partial\ell_k^\mu}
 \left(v^\mu\prod_jD_j^{-\sigma_j}\right)
 =\sum_u A_{\boldsymbol\sigma,o;u}(\boldsymbol x,d)
 I_{\boldsymbol a^{(u)}}.
 \label{eq:identity}
\end{equation}
The vector $v$ is a loop or external momentum, and $o$ labels the pair $(k,v)$. The coefficient $A_{\boldsymbol\sigma,o;u}$ multiplies the integral with index vector $\boldsymbol a^{(u)}$. Appendix~\ref{app:derivation} expands the derivative and derives the resulting index shifts. Several operators act on the same seed, so seed selection retains or removes a group of candidate equations.

Kira combines IBP identities with symmetry and Lorentz-invariance relations, removes redundant equations, and eliminates integrals in a chosen order. Back-substitution then expresses the requested targets in the remaining master basis~\cite{KiraOriginal,Kira3}. For target $T$, the result has the form
\begin{equation}
 T=\sum_{m=1}^{n_M}r_{Tm}(\boldsymbol x,d)M_m,
 \qquad r_{Tm}\in\Q(\boldsymbol x,d).
 \label{eq:reduction}
\end{equation}
Here $M_m$ is master integral $m$, $n_M$ is the number of masters, and $\Q(\boldsymbol x,d)$ is the field of rational functions with rational coefficients. Equation~\eqref{eq:reduction} follows by the exact row operations derived in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation}. It determines reduction coefficients; evaluating the masters is a further part of an amplitude calculation~\cite{Weinzierl2022}. Kira can use symbolic rational arithmetic or repeated finite-field solutions followed by rational reconstruction~\cite{Kira2}. We leave finite-field reconstruction unchanged in the native solver. The added recurrence obtains its rational coefficients directly.

Table~\ref{tab:places} locates the three learned decisions. Our implementation always obtains the coefficients through exact algebra. If a restricted system does not reach the supplied basis, it repeats the reduction with less restrictive settings, and the timer includes this recovery. Exactness follows from the retained identities and row operations; sufficiency must still be checked for each target, as explained in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation}.

\section{Training and comparison design}
\label{sec:design}
We use two kinds of labels. Early models learn membership in a recorded sufficient seed set or the next action in a recorded reduction. Such labels describe one valid history and do not establish a minimum number of seeds or operations. Later cost models learn from complete Kira timings for alternative choices. Section~\ref{sec:shared} uses this second objective to test whether one model can transfer across structures. The notes retain the reverse-construction dataset and its separate label-accuracy tests.

The earlier native paired comparisons use identical targets, kinematics, and final master bases. The connected-tree comparison reduces identical physical integrals into each method's selected basis. Its external check expresses the new unit-power master in the preceding basis and verifies every target coefficient; the application timer excludes that benchmark conversion. The native control disables learned choices in the same exact implementation. A fixed control uses a setting selected before testing and makes no request-dependent prediction. This second control measures what the prediction adds after fixed tuning. The earlier deployed application supplies a further practical comparison. When a study uses a modified executable, ``native'' means its native decision rules; it does not mean an unmodified Kira installation.

The earlier native comparisons keep $d$ symbolic and assign rational values to the other kinematic variables. The analytic comparisons also include symbolic masses and momentum invariants. Thus the exported coefficients are exact rational functions of $d$ and any remaining symbolic scales. Master discovery and an enlarged-region reference calculation precede the supplied-basis timer. The application receives the master indices, while reference coefficients enter only the final check. We report this preparation cost separately where measured. Adding it to a supplied-basis time describes a two-stage workflow, since the reference calculation already performs a reduction; it does not measure a faster first reduction from scratch.

The new comparisons start a fresh process for each measurement and include imports, model loading, prediction, IBP generation, elimination, exact coefficient comparison, retries, output, and process exit. Three repetitions rotate method order on the same worker for each request. Earlier retained comparisons use their stated timers and repetitions in Table~\ref{tab:retained}. For the seed, equation, and shared-model workloads, we sum the median time of each request and divide by the corresponding control total. The historical row comparison uses a geometric mean, as stated in Section~\ref{sec:seed-row}. Appendix~\ref{app:statistics} defines this ratio and its resampling interval. A nontrivial batch contains at least one target that reduces to a nonzero expression beyond an unchanged basis element. Failures remain in the coverage counts and receive no completed-time ratio.

\section{Seed selection and elimination-row selection}
\label{sec:seed-row}
\subsection{Learning a sparse seed region}
The sparse-seed model limits the distance between a seed's numerator powers and the numerator-power regions of the targets. Its boundary also depends on the seed rank and sector. We fit the boundary by running complete reductions, then train cost predictors to choose among candidate boundaries. Appendix~\ref{app:sparse} defines the geometry and objective. Trees and forests provide the candidate cost models~\cite{CART1984,Breiman2001Forest,Geurts2006ExtraTrees}; validation determines whether request-dependent prediction improves on a fixed sparse setting.

This retained comparison uses planar and nonplanar two-loop boxes with numerator ranks two through four, unit positive physical indices, rational kinematics, and symbolic $d$. Twelve training requests and ten validation requests per graph precede the reserved test. Validation retains a fixed boundary for the planar graph and enables a depth-two, 32-tree extremely randomized forest for the nonplanar graph. The 20 reserved requests have seven repetitions per method. All 560 timed reductions pass exact checks, with 5,600 coefficient comparisons. Table~\ref{tab:retained} shows 22.2\% less time than tuned native choices and 3.6\% less time than the fixed sparse control. These measurements test new parameter profiles within known graphs; they motivate, but do not establish, cross-family transfer.

\subsection{Why the final method retains native row choices}
The row model scores the allowed source rows of the current coefficient matrix. Its original network maps 132 inputs to 32 features, pools the available actions, and uses a 64-component hidden layer to produce a probability for each action. The inputs describe the pivot, nonzero entries, matrix size, and sampled cancellations. Exact arithmetic performs the selected row operation, as derived in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation}. The network learns the recorded next action through categorical cross-entropy; the notes give its full specification and the derivation of that loss.

In our live Kira comparison, the original row network takes 1.305 times the stock reduction time across 84 exactly checked trials. A later compiled policy trained on algebraic cost gives a geometric mean time ratio of 0.982 with repeat interval $[0.959,1.003]$. Since this interval includes equal time, that experiment supplies no reliable complete-time gain. We therefore omit learned row selection from the retained method and leave Kira's row choices active. The historical row measurements and their different controls remain in the notes.

\section{Selecting equations before coefficient construction}
\label{sec:equation}
Equation selection makes a finer choice within the seed region. A classification tree scores each seed--operator pair before Kira builds its symbolic coefficients. Its seventeen features describe loop and external-momentum counts, index counts, target number, seed powers, sector membership, distance to a target, and operator index. The score is the weighted positive fraction in the reached leaf, using the tree construction of Ref.~\cite{CART1984}. Appendix~\ref{app:equation-model} defines the features, weights, and threshold rule.

A positive training label means that the equation's set of participating integrals appears in Kira's selected system. This proxy does not identify a unique necessary equation. We collect 137,015 sampled records, including 39,285 positive labels, from 66 training batches. Shared and graph-specific trees use depths 6, 10, 14, or 18 and minimum leaf populations 4 or 16. Separate validation first retains high-recall candidates, then measures complete reduction cost. A graph uses a filter only when validation gives at least 5\% less time than the same tuned calculation with all ML disabled. The selected filter can retain an individual equation or all operators from a retained seed.

The reserved comparison contains 68 matched nontrivial batches from a 74-request grid at one through four loops. Four reference checks fail and two batches are identity-only. Filtering reduces summed median time from 153.937 to 146.586 seconds, or 4.8\%, with ratio interval $[0.946,0.959]$. On the 32 batches where validation enables filtering, time falls from 91.707 to 84.384 seconds, or 8.0\%. All 700 timed completions, including the identity-only batches, pass exact checks. Prediction and five recovery attempts remain inside the timer. These results use known graph structures and partly repeated target patterns.

A separate test changes the index batches within the enabled structures while keeping the model fixed. It retains only batches absent from training and validation, with at least one previously unseen index vector. All 24 requests complete, with 144 exact timed reductions and 5,106 coefficient comparisons. Their summed median time changes from 72.295 to 67.478 seconds, or 6.7\%, with ratio interval $[0.928,0.940]$. This index-transfer result supplies the equation-model entry in Table~\ref{tab:retained}; it remains separate from transfer to new graphs.

\section{A shared model for transfer across integral families}
\label{sec:shared}
The preceding gains depend on validated graph domains. We therefore train a shared model from structural quantities, with graph names, stored family signatures, and coordinate-position identifiers excluded from its inputs. Its 44 inputs summarize the family and targets through loop and external-momentum counts, propagator incidence, masses, positive and negative powers, sector occupancy, and normalized versions of these counts. These features have fixed length when the number of loops or propagators changes. Appendix~\ref{app:shared} specifies the representation and prediction equations.

For each training request, the model predicts the complete cost of ten seed configurations, including changed positive-power bounds, numerator-rank bounds, and Kira's subsector truncation. We fit logarithms of time ratios to native bounds and penalize incomplete attempts. These labels include recovery to less restrictive configurations. A decision margin requires a predicted improvement before changing a common anchor action. The anchor and margin are global; no graph-specific deployment rule enters this new model.

We reuse only recorded training costs and reserve the earlier validation costs for model selection. The distinct-mass vacuum family of Appendix~\ref{app:families-new}, nonplanar boxes, pentagons, and loop orders four and above are excluded from both sets. The remaining data contain 74 training and 51 validation requests. Candidate models include nine tree or forest configurations, two regularized linear regressions, and two neural networks. Five-fold validation keeps each ordered graph structure within one fold, and the model selection also considers the separate validation set. Training selects a neural network with two 48-component hidden layers and a margin of 0.05. The network has 5,002 fitted weights and biases. The runtime evaluates the saved weights directly, and its predictions agree with those of the training library within the numerical check tolerance. Appendix~\ref{app:shared} gives the loss, candidate settings, and selection rule.

The reserved physical profile is new. Its 110 requests span the defined one-mass and massive vacuum families, massless and massive two-point families, four-point ladders, boxes with bubble subgraphs, and three additional scattering structures. The declared loop range is one through twelve. This tests both new parameter values in training domains and transfer to the excluded families or higher orders. Appendix~\ref{app:families-new} gives the denominators, kinematics, and target construction. Each request has an explicit outcome; a model prediction for an unsupported native input is recorded without calling it a successful reduction.

\section{Overall interpretation}
\label{sec:overall-new}
The cancellation comparison in Section~\ref{sec:gamma-cancel} holds the connected master basis and output procedure fixed. Its reduction in complete time comes from exact factor cancellation and calculations shared by targets in the same batch. The separate native controls continue to determine the added effect of the trained choices.

The connected-tree calculation in Section~\ref{sec:loop-tree-results} extends the exact physical domain before further native work. Its proof identifies the allowed integrals, and its coverage table states what the application actually completes at each loop order. The fresh two-loop control then measures the retained ML contribution on work that still needs Kira. This division follows the mathematical structure of the integrals and keeps the sources of time savings separate.

The batch comparison in Section~\ref{sec:analytic-batch-results} separates three changes: a checked master-index registry, an exact triangle formula, and a learned choice of target grouping and sparse seed region. The same-basis control isolates the latter two changes. The fixed-action control then measures the added batch model under the same exact implementation, while the control with both learned choices disabled measures its combination with the retained sparse expert. The preceding formula and recurrence experiments remain separate comparisons with their original inputs and controls.

The direct local comparisons establish savings for seed and equation decisions within their measured domains (Table~\ref{tab:retained}). The shared-model comparison tests the additional question of transfer without a family-specific rule (Section~\ref{sec:general-results}). These are separate workloads and controls, so their ratios do not form a cumulative speedup. Learned row selection remains omitted because its measured gain is not resolved from timing variation. The combined application uses the validated sparse expert together with the exact methods in Sections~\ref{sec:blocks13} and~\ref{sec:analytic-method}. The shared model and equation filter retain their separately tested applications, and their gains are not added to the hybrid result.

The preceding complete application combined validated learned choices with exact preparation changes. On 41 matched batches it took 56.461 seconds against 66.045 seconds for native choices, a ratio of 0.855. Its isolated new learned choice changed the seed action in only one batch. We retain that earlier experiment in the notes because its combined gain does not establish a general learned policy. Exact sector-storage changes, scaling reuse, and analytic scalar recurrences likewise remain distinct from learned gains. In particular, the scalar recurrence checks through twelve loops verify a restricted exact formula, not a high-loop ML reduction.
