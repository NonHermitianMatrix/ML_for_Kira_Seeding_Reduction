\section{Introduction}
Feynman integrals arise from integration over internal loop momenta in higher-order perturbative corrections to scattering amplitudes and particle decay amplitudes. Many integrals in a calculation share propagators and differ only in their powers or numerators. Integration-by-parts (IBP) identities relate these integrals and express them in terms of a smaller set of master integrals~\cite{Weinzierl2022,ChetyrkinTkachov}. This decomposition supplies the coefficients needed to assemble an amplitude; the evaluation of any remaining master integrals is a subsequent calculation~\cite{Weinzierl2022}.

Kira automates the generation and solution of the IBP system, and its current methods already remove unnecessary seeds and equations~\cite{KiraOriginal,Kira3}. The remaining cost depends on the integral structure and the requested targets. Generating fewer useful equations can reduce work, but a restricted system can also fail to reach the required basis and require a larger calculation. We therefore optimize complete reduction time, including prediction, algebra, and recovery. Seed-count reduction alone does not measure this objective.

Our method first identifies structures that admit an exact reduction formula. It applies those formulas to complete requests, individual targets, and recognized intermediate sectors. For the remaining supported native calculations, we retain the seed policy and compare learned selection of symbolic or finite-field algebra with a fixed setting for each propagator structure. Exact algebra determines every output coefficient. Related machine-assisted searches motivate the seed intervention~\cite{Song2025,TubeSeeding2026}, while analytic one-loop reduction motivates the formula stage~\cite{DennerDittmaier2006,Hu2025}. We derive the particular formulas and cost objective used here in the appendices.

The method combines exact partial fractions, tensor and scalar recurrences, and analytic values for specific connected trees. We then address the remaining native coefficient algebra with finite-field reconstruction and a trained cost selector. A matched comparison separates the direct formulas, the established reconstruction algorithm, and the added learned decision. Earlier equation-selection and row-selection results remain separate, and the companion notes preserve their full experiments.

\section{The decomposition problem and Kira}
\label{sec:physics}
We first define the notation used in this paper. An integral family specifies inverse propagators, masses, and external scalar products. In dimensional regularization, we write~\cite{Weinzierl2022,KiraOriginal}
\begin{equation}
 I_{\boldsymbol a}(\boldsymbol x,d)=\int\prod_{h=1}^{L}
 \frac{d^d\ell_h}{i\pi^{d/2}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}D_j^{-a_j},
 \qquad D_j=Q_j^2-m_j^2+i0,\qquad d=4-2\epsilon.
 \label{eq:integral}
\end{equation}
Here $L$ is the number of loops, $\ell_h$ is loop momentum $h$, $N$ is the number of integral coordinates, and $Q_j$ is a specified linear combination of loop and external momenta. The quantity $m_j$ is the mass on line $j$, $\boldsymbol x$ contains squared masses and external invariants, $\epsilon$ is the dimensional regulator, and $i0$ specifies the Feynman pole prescription. A positive integer $a_j$ gives a denominator power, zero removes that factor, and a negative integer gives a numerator power. Auxiliary inverse propagators complete the coordinates for loop scalar products when the physical lines do not suffice~\cite{KiraOriginal}.

A sector consists of integrals with the same positive-index positions. Removing a positive position gives a sub-sector. A seed $\boldsymbol\sigma$ is an index vector at which we generate identities~\cite{KiraOriginal}. For a loop or external momentum $v$, the IBP relation is~\cite{ChetyrkinTkachov}
\begin{equation}
 0=\int\prod_h\frac{d^d\ell_h}{i\pi^{d/2}}
 \frac{\partial}{\partial\ell_k^\mu}
 \left(v^\mu\prod_jD_j^{-\sigma_j}\right)
 =\sum_u A_{\boldsymbol\sigma,o;u}(\boldsymbol x,d)
 I_{\boldsymbol a^{(u)}}.
 \label{eq:identity}
\end{equation}
The operator label $o$ specifies $(k,v)$, and $A_{\boldsymbol\sigma,o;u}$ is the coefficient of integral $u$. Appendix~\ref{app:derivation} expands the derivative into index shifts and explains why the coefficients are rational. Each seed supplies several operators and therefore several equations.

Kira orders integrals, uses symmetries and other valid relations, selects equations, and performs elimination and back-substitution~\cite{KiraOriginal,Kira3,Laporta}. For a target $T$, the result has the form
\begin{equation}
 T=\sum_{m=1}^{n_M}r_{Tm}(\boldsymbol x,d)M_m,
 \qquad r_{Tm}\in\Q(\boldsymbol x,d).
 \label{eq:reduction}
\end{equation}
Here $M_m$ is master integral $m$, $n_M$ is the number of masters, and $\Q(\boldsymbol x,d)$ is the field of rational functions with rational coefficients. Exact row operations give Eq.~\eqref{eq:reduction}, as derived in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation}. A remaining free integral in a restricted system does not by itself establish a master of the full family. We check completion against a reference basis. Kira can solve symbolically or use finite fields and reconstruct rational functions~\cite{Kira2}; this study leaves its reconstruction algorithm unchanged.

The original three learned interventions occur at distinct points (Table~\ref{tab:places}). A seed policy changes which identities are generated. An equation policy selects seed--operator pairs. A row policy chooses among allowed elimination steps after coefficients exist. All three can change cost without predicting the physical coefficients themselves. The present extension adds a model that selects the coefficient-algebra procedure for the remaining native reductions.

\section{Exact reductions before native elimination}
\label{sec:exact}
The exact stage uses the propagator structure to determine whether a formula applies. For independent loop components, the product measure and separated integrand factorize Eq.~\eqref{eq:integral}; Appendix~\ref{app:derivation} proves the resulting product of component reductions. Identical components share their calculation. Connected graphs require their own identities, so a high loop count alone does not select this shortcut.

Table~\ref{tab:exact-domains} summarizes the retained domains. For generic one-loop sectors, we resolve numerator scalar products along the external directions and average the perpendicular components. This yields scalar integrals whose raised powers are lowered by an exact IBP recurrence. Appendix~\ref{app:tensor-reduction} derives both steps. Their coefficients are rational in $d$, and keeping known linear dimension factors separate reduces repeated symbolic simplification. A singular matrix can prevent those particular recurrences from being used, which motivates the new two-point rule.

\subsection{Two-point sectors at zero external invariant}
Let $D_1=\ell^2-x$ and $D_2=(\ell-p)^2-y$, where $x$ and $y$ are squared masses and $p^2=0$. Write $T(x)$ for the unit-power tadpole with squared mass $x$. Feynman parametrization gives
\begin{equation}
 B_{11}(x,y)=\frac{T(x)-T(y)}{x-y},\qquad
 B_{ab}(x,y)=\frac{\partial_x^{a-1}\partial_y^{b-1}B_{11}(x,y)}{(a-1)!(b-1)!}.
 \label{eq:light-main}
\end{equation}
Here $a$ and $b$ are positive propagator powers, and derivatives treat the two masses as independent before any specialization. Appendix~\ref{app:lightlike} derives this relation, its equal-mass limit, and the finite tensor formula. At equal masses, $B_{ab}(x,x)=T_{a+b}(x)$, where $T_n$ is the tadpole with denominator power $n$. A zero-mass tadpole vanishes in dimensional regularization~\cite{Weinzierl2022}.

For polynomial numerators, the same parameter shift leaves one radial denominator. Angular moments reduce the numerator to a finite sum, and a change from the Feynman parameter to the interpolated squared mass expresses each term through the two endpoint tadpoles. The result is a rational coefficient of $T(x)$ and $T(y)$, with no inverse external Gram matrix. This also applies when a generic one-loop recurrence reaches such a two-line sub-sector. Applying the identity at that intermediate step extends the complete reduction beyond requests that begin as bubbles.

The application tests the stated conditions before using a formula. It retains native reduction for unsupported targets, symbolic numerators outside the accepted domain, and recurrence limits. A request can contain both accepted and remaining targets; the application combines their exact outputs. These checks determine mathematical applicability. They do not ask a learned model to certify an identity.

\subsection{Direct vacuum evaluation}
A massless vacuum family has no dimensional scale and vanishes in dimensional regularization~\cite{Weinzierl2022}. We apply this check before any elimination, including to coupled loop momenta. Appendix~\ref{app:direct-vacuum} gives the radial scaling argument and the implemented conditions.

When every denominator in a component carries the same loop momentum, its integrand is a rational function of that momentum squared. Equal masses combine their powers, and partial fractions express the remaining poles through raised-power tadpoles. Appendix~\ref{app:direct-vacuum} derives the coefficients and evaluates each tadpole through Gamma functions. Polynomial terms and massless vacuum factors vanish in dimensional regularization~\cite{Weinzierl2022}. The application therefore returns both a master decomposition and an explicit analytic value for this domain.

This rule also handles repeated masses and polynomial numerators. It applies independently to each separated loop factor and computes identical components once. Recognition requires the absence of a momentum coupling between factors. Connected graphs use a separate identity.

\subsection{Connected trees and higher loop counts}
Successive massless bubble integrations also reduce specific connected trees to a unit-power integral of the same tree. For a massless two-point root, the coefficient is a rational function of $d$ and the external invariant. For a massive vacuum root, it is rational in $d$ and the one squared mass. Appendix~\ref{app:trees} derives these statements from Gamma-function products and their integer shifts. Equation~\eqref{eq:direct-tree-value} gives the complete analytic value of a connected vacuum tree, and the application now returns this value with its reduction. This family includes connected high-loop integrals, while independent products provide a separate high-loop test. Neither construction represents general coupled multiscale scattering.

\section{Learning the remaining Kira cost}
\label{sec:learning}
Exact formulas remove some reductions from the native calculation. For the remaining integrals, the cost still depends on the seed region and on how the coefficients are represented during elimination. We keep the previously validated seed policy and train a new model to choose the algebra procedure. This separates a change in the equations from a change in the arithmetic used to solve them.

\subsection{Seed, equation, and row decisions}
The retained seed model chooses among the wide, tight, and medium regions of Appendix~\ref{app:sparse}. Its cost tree has maximum depth two, and its failure guard uses twelve depth-three trees per action. The original training used 34 batches and sixteen validation batches. We preserve those weights during the present algebra comparison. The companion notes describe that training and a later unsuccessful replacement using complete seed regions.

The separately tested equation model ranks seed--operator pairs before their full coefficients are constructed. It reduces time on its recorded workload, but the present combined application keeps this filter off. The original next-row model scores allowed elimination steps in the changing matrix. Its additional cost exceeded the algebraic savings in the retained comparison, and a later compiled policy did not establish a consistent saving. We omit learned row selection and retain the native elimination rules. Section~\ref{sec:results} gives the separate controls for these three interventions.

\subsection{Choosing the algebra procedure}
Direct symbolic elimination carries rational functions through each operation. Kira can instead solve the equations at finite-field points and use FireFly to reconstruct the rational functions~\cite{Kira2,FireFly2019,FireFly2020}. The preceding native executable uses symbolic reduction without FireFly support. The new executable retains the same project changes to Kira and adds its standard FireFly capability. We use that reconstruction algorithm without changing its mathematics. Our added decision selects direct symbolic reduction or full reconstruction with a probe batch size of 1, 2, 4, 8, or 16. The probe batch size is the number of numerical evaluations grouped by the native calculation. It differs from the number of target integrals in a request.

We prepare 48 training requests, 22 validation requests, and 22 reserved native requests. They contain planar and nonplanar two-loop boxes, massive two-loop sunrise integrals, and a massive three-loop vacuum family. Box batches contain up to six targets with numerator rank at most four. Additional requests test the sector corner and raised propagator powers. The full physical inputs are disjoint between splits; graph structures and some individual target vectors recur. Appendix~\ref{app:reconstruction-inputs} specifies the propagators, targets, kinematics, and split.

Each input has 29 features derived before reduction. They describe loop and external-momentum counts, propagator incidence, masses, sectors, target powers, and the size of the rational kinematic numbers. Family names do not enter the learned features. Appendix~\ref{app:shared} defines the features and training objective. Training measures one complete application per action, and validation measures three. Failed trials retain their elapsed cost and an estimated recovery cost from the measured symbolic control.

We fit ensembles of 32 extremely randomized regression trees~\cite{Geurts2006ExtraTrees}. Each ensemble predicts relative complete cost and an observed failure fraction for all six actions. Separate validation selects the depth, minimum leaf population, and required predicted saving. A fixed control also selects its best action separately for each propagator structure. Thus the learned model must improve on a structure-dependent setting. Deployment requires at least three percent less validation cost and no failed selected validation attempt. Inputs outside the measured feature ranges use the preceding application, and an unsuccessful reconstruction falls back to its symbolic procedure.

The model selects a method before Kira generates and solves the equations. It supplies no reduction coefficients. Exact formulas retain their mathematical admission checks, and the native comparison checks every returned rational coefficient against a separate symbolic reduction in the same master basis. Reconstruction and basis admission alone do not constitute a formal proof for every new input. The independent coefficient checks support the tested results.

\section{Comparison design}
\label{sec:design}
We fix the trained weights, selected settings, procedures, and reserved inputs before the final comparison. The native test contains 22 new physical requests. A separate analytic test contains 53 previously verified requests: thirteen two-mass vacuum products, 34 connected one-mass vacuum trees, three zero-invariant tensor bubbles, and three ordinary one-loop tensor requests. The two vacuum grids cover every loop order from one through thirteen. These analytic requests test preservation of the exact methods and are excluded from the new model's training. The companion notes also retain the preceding 26-request repeated-mass and scaleless coverage test.

Four applications process the same requests. The earlier application precedes the direct vacuum additions. The preceding application includes those formulas and the retained native seed model. The fixed application adds a validated algebra setting for each propagator structure. The integrated application enables the new cost model only if validation admits it. The fixed and integrated applications share all exact formulas, native settings, and preceding seed decisions. Their comparison isolates the additional algebra-selection model. Comparing the integrated and earlier applications measures the cumulative change across these two extensions on one matched workload.

A subsequent paired comparison uses the same 22 reserved native requests to isolate the inherited learned choices. It holds the validated reconstruction settings fixed and compares the retained learned procedure with its existing all-fixed procedure. Both use the same protected bases, exact references, and resource bounds. This comparison performs no additional fitting or selection from reserved outcomes. Its timings are measured together and are reported separately from the first four-method experiment.

Each timed application starts a fresh process. Its elapsed time includes imports, input preparation, model loading and prediction, exact formulas, native equation generation and solution, any recovery, output, and exit. Reference preparation and the external coefficient comparison lie outside this timer. We run one computational thread per application, use three repetitions, and rotate method order on the same worker. An application has a 90-second outer limit; the bounded native runner can impose an earlier limit on an individual attempt. All methods use the same protected master basis where the native reduction requires one.

Vacuum-product references use independent symbolic partial fractions. The connected-tree comparison checks the preceding exact rule, supported by the derivation in Appendix~\ref{app:trees}. Native and tensor references use separate symbolic Kira calculations. Reference preparation records unsuccessful attempts separately from failures inside a timed application. A request counts as complete only if all repetitions agree with the corresponding exact reference. We report failure and completion counts beside time ratios.

The primary time estimator is the ratio of summed per-request median times on the matched completed subset. Appendix~\ref{app:statistics} defines it and the paired request-resampling interval. This measures the sampled collection, so results for distinct workloads are not multiplied into a general speed factor. The four-method comparison provides the cumulative result directly.
