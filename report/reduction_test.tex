\section{Tests of the Reduction-Propagation Model}
\label{sec:reduction-test}
To test the model in Section~\ref{sec:reduction-model}, we used the
parameters saved after thirty training passes and evaluated them on
the 72,142 targets in the 307 reserved families. We tested
the next choice at a recorded matrix and the complete sequence obtained
when the model receives the matrix produced by its own preceding choice.
We used the saved parameters and raw output probabilities throughout
this evaluation. The same reserved set previously measured the
ten-pass model, so these results give a repeated evaluation after the
specified twenty-pass continuation. The reserved records remained
outside gradient updates, and the new evaluation changed no parameters,
training settings or confidence map.
The accompanying records retain the predictions, chosen identities
and resulting coefficients~\cite{ReductionModelTestData}.

\subsection{Test inputs and next-step agreement}
The test contains 16,124 individual integrals and 56,018
integral expressions. There are 306 one-loop families and one two-loop
family, the massless propagator family in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:generated-two-loop-propagator}. Their recorded reductions
contain 247,422 states, of which 194,347 have at least two
allowed steps and 53,075 have one forced step. Every target stays
in the family division defined in Section~\ref{sec:seed-model-split}.

For each recorded state, the model receives the row before the step
and all its admissible source equations. We compare the selected
action $\widehat a_i$ with the recorded action $a_i^\star$, where $i$
labels the state. Let $\mathcal Q$ be the set of states with at least
two actions and $N_{\mathcal Q}=|\mathcal Q|$. Their next-step agreement is
\begin{equation}
 \mathrm{Acc}_{1}=\frac{1}{N_{\mathcal Q}}
       \sum_{i\in\mathcal Q}\mathbf1[\widehat a_i=a_i^\star].
 \label{eq:reduction-test-agreement}
\end{equation}
The indicator $\mathbf1$ equals one when its stated condition holds
and zero otherwise. Forced steps have probability one without a learned
choice, so the primary metrics exclude them. We also check equality of
the sampled next rows, since different equations can give the same update.

The model matched 94.716\% of recorded choices, placed the recorded
choice among its three highest scores in 99.993\% of states, and
produced the same next sampled row in 94.727\% of states.
Mean agreement per target with a choice was 95.937\%, and the
mean across families with choices was 95.357\%.
Figure~\ref{fig:reduction-action-accuracy} compares these state-level
results with a uniform random choice and the fixed local rule defined
below. The random-choice value averages $1/|\mathcal A_i|$ over the same
states, where $\mathcal A_i$ is the supplied action set.

\subsection{Confidence and variation between families}
Let $p_i(a)$ be the probability assigned to action $a$ in state $i$.
Its largest value $p_i^{\max}$ is the selected-action confidence from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:reduction-choice}, and
$c_i=\mathbf1[\widehat a_i=a_i^\star]$ records agreement with the
reference action.
We compare their means in ten equal intervals of $[0,1]$, including
confidence one in the last interval. The empirical calibration error is
\begin{equation}
 E_{\rm cal}=\sum_{\beta:N_\beta>0}
       \frac{N_\beta}{N_{\mathcal Q}}
       |\overline p_\beta^{\max}-\overline c_\beta|,
 \label{eq:reduction-test-calibration}
\end{equation}
where $N_\beta$ is the number of choice states in interval $\beta$ and
the overbars denote their means. This follows the reliability-bin
approach of Ref.~\cite{GuoCalibration} without fitting a calibration map.

We also compare the full probability distribution with the recorded
choice. The mean negative log probability and multiclass Brier
score are
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 L_{\rm test}&=-\frac{1}{N_{\mathcal Q}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal Q}
        \log\!\left[\max\{p_i(a_i^\star),10^{-38}\}\right],\\
 B_{\rm test}&=\frac{1}{N_{\mathcal Q}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal Q}
        \sum_{a\in\mathcal A_i}
        \left(p_i(a)-\mathbf1[a=a_i^\star]\right)^2 .
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:reduction-test-probability-scores}
\end{equation}
The probability floor defines the logarithm if a returned probability
rounds to zero. Mean confidence was 92.199\%, compared with
94.716\% agreement, while $E_{\rm cal}=0.0252$,
$L_{\rm test}=0.1105$ and $B_{\rm test}=0.0783$.
Figure~\ref{fig:reduction-confidence} shows agreement and state counts
within each interval. These probabilities concern the recorded next
choice and do not give a probability that the whole reduction succeeds.

Exact ties in the largest returned probability occurred in 5.618\%
of choice states. The implemented rule resolves them by equation
identifier. Choosing uniformly among tied maxima would give
91.920\% next-step agreement. We retain the implemented rule
in all predictions and paths; the additional count separates score
discrimination from tie resolution.

\subsection{Complete reduction sequences and coefficient checks}
Agreement at a recorded state measures a single decision. To check how
successive decisions affect the reduction, we also start from each target
and let the model select every step. After each exact update, it receives
the row that its own choice produced. The next pivot is the largest
remaining nonmaster integral under Eq.~\eqref{eq:target-reference-order}.
We obtain the candidate equations from the family's stored identities
with that leading integral and a nonzero sampled pivot. Rebuilding this
set reproduces every candidate list along the recorded reference paths.

For comparison, a fixed local rule tries the same candidate equations
and selects the result with the fewest nonmaster terms. It resolves
ties first by the total number of nonzero terms, then by the arithmetic
count $1+2n_q$ for a source row with $n_q$ nonzero coefficients, and
finally by equation identifier. Both policies receive the same target,
master basis, source equations and integral order. Neither policy
receives the final master coefficients or later reference steps.

A sequence ends when the row contains only masters. We also stop if
no supplied equation can cancel the next integral, if the row exceeds
4,096 nonzero terms, or if the sequence reaches 4,096 updates.
The model's input bounds in Section~\ref{sec:reduction-model} also apply.
These bounds prevent an unsuccessful sequence from growing without
limit. We retain every unsuccessful target in the results and supply
no reference-path fallback.

Each selected update uses exact finite-field arithmetic and passes
a cross-multiplied row check. We then repeat its sequence of source
identities at all three stored points and primes, evaluating the
original symbolic coefficients at each point. A vanishing source
pivot marks an unverified sequence at that point. Successful
verification requires a completed reduction and equality with every
stored master coefficient at all three samples. These checks use the
finite-field approach discussed in Section~\ref{sec:ibp-reduction}%
~\cite{Kira2}. Agreement at the sampled points supplies evidence for
these numerical reductions; a symbolic result at generic parameters
would require additional reconstruction or symbolic verification.

The learned policy passed all three coefficient comparisons for
72,088 of 72,142 targets (99.925\%), while the local rule
passed for 71,463 targets (99.059\%).
The remaining 54 learned paths stopped at a row with
no allowed next action. Every completed learned path matched all three
samples, and all attempts stayed within the prescribed size and step bounds. The local rule stopped with no
allowed action for 664 targets. It matched the first
sample for 71,478 targets, of which 15
failed the additional coefficient comparisons. These cases show why
a completed reduction at one sample requires checks at other points.

An allowed identity can introduce a
smaller nonmaster integral for which the stored pool has no usable
source equation. The row update remains valid, while the supplied
equations cannot continue that selected sequence. In the two-loop
family, the learned
policy passed 144 of 144 targets and the local rule
passed 144. Figure~\ref{fig:reduction-rollout-success}
separates the loop orders and compares the first-sample results with
the three-sample results.

\subsection{Operation counts and measured reduction time}
To measure the work along a completed path, we count one division
and one multiplication and subtraction per nonzero source coefficient,
as in Section~\ref{sec:integrals-generation}. If $K$ is the number of
selected steps and $n_{q_i}$ is the number of nonzero coefficients
in the source selected at step $i$, the committed operation count is
\begin{equation}
 C_{\rm path}=\sum_{i=1}^{K}(1+2n_{q_i}).
 \label{eq:reduction-test-path-cost}
\end{equation}
Both policies also make trial updates to compare candidates. Their
corresponding count is
\begin{equation}
 C_{\rm trial}=\sum_{i=1}^{K}
                   \sum_{a\in\mathcal A_i}(1+2n_{q(a)}),
 \label{eq:reduction-test-trial-cost}
\end{equation}
where $q(a)$ is the source equation of candidate $a$ and $\mathcal A_i$
is the action set at step $i$. We report $C_{\rm path}$ and
$C_{\rm path}+C_{\rm trial}$ separately. These counts cover row
arithmetic and exclude neural-network calculations, row checks,
equation generation, symbolic simplification and reconstruction.

The recorded construction gives a valid reference count $C_{\rm ref}$
under the same arithmetic convention. Its minimality has not been
established, so a ratio $C_{\rm path}/C_{\rm ref}$ measures cost relative
to this recorded path. Among successful learned paths with
$C_{\rm ref}>0$, 72 used fewer committed operations,
71,793 used the same number, and 223 used more.
For the 71,440 targets that both policies completed and that
have $C_{\rm ref}>0$, the learned policy used fewer operations than
the local rule in 233 cases, the same number in
64,708 cases, and more in 6,499 cases.

Figure~\ref{fig:reduction-operations} uses this common set so that both
curves describe the same targets. It shows how the trial updates
add work even when the selected path has the same operation count
as the reference. The separate success comparison retains the
targets excluded from these completed-path ratios.

We measured each complete attempted sequence on one CPU, including
state construction, candidate selection, exact updates and the
per-step row checks. The measurement excludes file access, building
the shared source-equation pool and the subsequent replays at the
stored samples. We alternated the order in which the two policies
ran from one target to the next. The mean times over all targets,
including unsuccessful attempts, were 10.0639\,ms for the
learned policy and 0.4643\,ms for the local rule, giving
a ratio of 21.68. The corresponding medians were
8.2534\,ms and 0.3625\,ms.
Figure~\ref{fig:reduction-runtime} shows the distribution and its
summary values. These measurements include the current model's
feature construction and scoring cost and describe this target-row
implementation. The elapsed times come from a single cluster run and
include variation in node load; repeated runs would be needed to
estimate timing uncertainty.

\subsection{Scope of the test results}
The learned policy completed more targets than the fixed local rule
and had a higher mean measured reduction time. The present implementation therefore provides no speedup over this local rule.
The operation-count comparison above measures the cost of the completed
paths, while the failure counts identify targets whose supplied equations
did not support the sequence selected by the policy.
The earlier ten-pass model, evaluated on the same reserved set, matched
95.469\% of recorded choices and completed 72,026 targets with agreement
at all three samples~\cite{ReductionModelTenEpochTestData}. After
thirty passes, the corresponding results are 94.716\% and
72,088 targets. The additional training therefore increased the
number of completed reductions while lowering agreement with recorded
next choices. These measures describe different aspects of the reduction:
a different valid next choice can still lead to the required masters.

The confidence comparison concerns agreement with a recorded action.
A valid alternative can have a different equation identifier or
intermediate row, which is why the complete coefficient checks
accompany the choice metrics.

The test covers one changing target row reduced with fixed source
identities. Most reserved families have the same one-loop box
topology with different mass and kinematic conditions, and some
lower-sector structures occur in both training and testing.
The two-loop result comes from one reserved propagator family.
These measurements therefore establish performance within this
collection and supplied action rule. Reductions of larger evolving
matrices, additional high-loop families and complete Kira jobs
require separate measurements.
